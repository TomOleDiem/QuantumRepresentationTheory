5 The hydrogen atom

This is the payoff chapter, and where everything above gets stitched together. Besides ordinary angular momentum \(\vec J\), a classical particle orbiting under an inverse-square force conserves a second vector quantity, the Runge–Lenz vector, which points along the orbit’s major axis; quantum mechanically it survives as an operator \(\vec A\) on the bound-state eigenspaces. Together \(\vec J\) and (a rescaled) \(\vec A\) generate a bigger symmetry algebra than rotations alone, and — remarkably — it splits as two commuting copies of the angular-momentum algebra from Chapter 4, \(\vec I = \tfrac 12(\vec J+\vec A)\) and \(\vec K = \tfrac 12(\vec J-\vec A)\). Feeding this pair into the classification of commuting \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\)-actions (§2.3) and the two Casimir elements it carries (Chapter 3) is what forces the two copies to have equal spin and produces, with no reference to the radial Schrödinger equation, the characteristic \(n^2\)-fold degenerate, \(-1/n^2\)-spaced hydrogen spectrum. As throughout, this chapter is a self-contained algebraic model: it takes the existence of a finite-dimensional bound-state eigenspace with the right operators on it as given, rather than deriving it from Physlib’s Hamiltonian (see the note on Physlib in the Overview).

\(\varepsilon _{ijk}\), the totally antisymmetric symbol on \(\{ 1,2,3\} \) with \(\varepsilon _{123}=1\), used to write the cross product and the commutation relations below.

Caveat: Mathlib already has a bilinear cross product on Fin 3 → R (crossProduct, in Mathlib.LinearAlgebra.CrossProduct), with bilinearity built into its type (it is a LinearMap.mk₂) rather than proved separately. Reusing it in place of this from-scratch Levi-Civita definition, so that Theorem 54 below becomes free, is a natural simplification not yet made — see the caveats chapter.
